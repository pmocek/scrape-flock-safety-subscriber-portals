Overview

Stamping Ground KY PD uses Flock Safety technology to capture objective evidence without compromising on individual privacy. Stamping Ground KY PD utilizes retroactive search to solve crimes after they've occurred. Additionally, Stamping Ground KY PD utilizes real time alerting of hotlist vehicles to capture wanted criminals. In an effort to ensure proper usage and guardrails are in place, they have made the below policies and usage statistics available to the public.

Myth vs. Fact
Myth

Flock has been hacked.

Fact

Flock's cloud environment has never been breached. Flock invests in a strong security posture, including adhering to NDAA, SOC2 (Type II), ISO 27001, ISO 27018 FedRAMP 20x, and Higher Education Community Vendor Assessment Tool (HECVAT) compliance. Additional security precautions include Role-Based Access Controls, data encrypted at-rest and in transit, required Multi-Factor Authentication, and regular penetration testing.

Myth

Flock sells and/or shares customer data.

Fact

Your community owns 100% of your data and it will never be sold. Only your agency decides who to share your data with, not Flock. Flock Safety does not publicly release agency search data. Flock does not operate or support any public-facing searchable database of law enforcement lookups, and we do not provide license plate search histories to websites or third parties without the agency's knowledge and authorization. Myth

My data is fed into a national database.

Fact

Each agency controls access to its own data. Agencies may choose to share information through a broader network, but nothing is shared automatically. Participation is opt-in, governed by defined agreements, and limited to lawful investigative purposes.

Myth

Flock cameras use facial recognition.

Fact

Flock cameras do not use facial recognition technology. Searches are based on vehicle characteristics, and not identity. Flock cannot recognize, identify, or track individuals. It is purpose built for specific public safety investigations, not to monitor people.

Stamping Ground Police Department ALPR Policy

SGPD #28- AUTOMATED LICENSE PLATE READERS (ALPR)
This policy is for internal use only and does not enlarge an employee’s civil liability in
any way. The policy should not be construed as creating a higher duty of care, in an
evidentiary sense, with respect to third-party civil claims against employees. A violation
of this policy, if proven, can only form the basis of a complaint by this agency for
non-judicial administrative action in accordance with the laws governing employee
discipline.
Date Implemented: 07/01/2023 Revision Date: 08/27/2026
1. PURPOSE
The purpose of this policy is to establish lawful, accountable, and constitutionally sound
procedures governing the deployment and use of Automated License Plate Recognition (ALPR)
technology by the Stamping Ground Police Department.
ALPR technology shall be used as an investigative and public-safety tool and shall not be used as
a general-purpose system for continuously monitoring individuals.
2. POLICY STATEMENT
The Department recognizes that ALPR technology records vehicles traveling in public locations
at specific points in time.
An ALPR camera does not continuously track a vehicle, does not receive a vehicle's GPS
location, and does not identify the driver merely because a vehicle is photographed.
ALPR information shall be used only for legitimate law-enforcement and public-safety purposes
authorized by Kentucky law and this policy.
3. AUTHORIZED USES
ALPR technology may be used for:
● identifying stolen vehicles;
● locating vehicles associated with wanted persons;
● locating vehicles associated with missing or endangered persons;
● investigating criminal offenses;
● developing investigative leads;
● locating vehicles associated with serious or violent crimes;
● identifying vehicles associated with an active criminal investigation;
● assisting in the recovery of stolen property or vehicles;
● responding to alerts from authorized law-enforcement databases; and
● other purposes expressly authorized by Kentucky law.
4. PROHIBITED USE
ALPR information shall not be searched or used:
● for personal reasons;
● out of curiosity;
● to monitor friends, relatives, neighbors, or acquaintances;
● to monitor political activity;
● to monitor religious activity;
● to monitor constitutionally protected speech or assembly;
● to monitor journalists solely because of their occupation;
● to monitor attorneys solely because of their occupation;
● to conduct generalized surveillance of law-abiding persons; or
● for any purpose not authorized by law or this policy.
5. INVESTIGATIVE SEARCHES
An officer conducting a historical ALPR search shall have a legitimate law-enforcement purpose.
When reasonably practicable, the officer shall document:
1. the associated case number or incident number;
2. the investigative purpose;
3. the search parameters;
4. the date of the search; and
5. the identity of the officer conducting the search.
A search shall be reasonably tailored to the investigation.
Broad searches unrelated to an identifiable law-enforcement purpose are prohibited.
6. VEHICLE INFORMATION VERSUS PERSON INFORMATION
ALPR information shall be understood as information concerning a vehicle observed at a
particular location and time.
An ALPR observation does not, by itself, establish:
● who was driving;
● who was inside the vehicle;
● where the occupants originated;
● where the occupants were going;
● what occurred between camera observations; or
● the identity of any person associated with the vehicle.
Officers shall not represent an ALPR observation as proof that a particular person was present
unless additional evidence establishes that fact.
7. ALPR ALERTS
An ALPR alert shall be considered an investigative lead.
Before conducting a traffic stop based upon an ALPR alert, the officer shall comply with
Kentucky law and visually confirm that:
1. the license plate observed by the officer matches the plate associated with the alert; and
2. the circumstances provide an appropriate lawful basis for the stop.
An ALPR alert alone shall not be treated as conclusive proof of criminal conduct.
8. RETENTION
ALPR data shall not be retained beyond the period authorized by Kentucky law.
Captured ALPR data shall generally be destroyed after 90 days unless a statutory exception
applies, including lawful retention for a criminal investigation, use as evidence, auditing,
qualifying training, subpoena, or preservation of evidence.
The Department shall maintain procedures ensuring that retained evidence is handled under the
Department's applicable evidence and records-retention policies.
9. ACCESS AND SECURITY
Access to ALPR data shall be limited to authorized personnel.
User credentials shall not be shared.
Each user shall be individually identifiable within the system.
Officers shall not access the ALPR system using another person's credentials.
Unauthorized access shall be investigated and may result in disciplinary action.
10. THIRD-PARTY DATA SHARING
ALPR information shall not be sold, transferred, or shared except as authorized by Kentucky
law.
Requests from outside agencies shall be handled in accordance with Kentucky law and
Department procedures.
The Department shall maintain documentation of significant external data requests and
disclosures when required by law or Department policy.
11. SUPERVISORY REVIEW
Supervisors shall be responsible for ensuring that personnel use the ALPR system in accordance
with this policy.
Supervisors shall review questionable, unusually broad, or potentially improper searches.
12. AUDITING
The Department shall conduct an ALPR audit at least every 90 days as required by Kentucky
law.
The audit shall include, when reasonably available:
● user activity;
● search activity;
● search purposes;
● unauthorized access attempts;
● data-sharing activity;
● retention compliance;
● policy compliance; and
● corrective actions.
Audit results shall be maintained in accordance with applicable records-retention requirements.
13. TRAINING
Personnel shall receive training before being authorized to use the ALPR system.
Training shall include:
● system operation;
● Kentucky ALPR law;
● Fourth Amendment principles;
● Kentucky Constitution Section 10;
● appropriate investigative use;
● prohibited searches;
● data security;
● retention requirements;
● alert verification; and
● consequences of misuse.
14. CONSTITUTIONAL SAFEGUARD FOR EXTENSIVE HISTORICAL
SEARCHES
The Department recognizes that an extensive historical search capable of producing a detailed
and prolonged reconstruction of an individual's movements may present constitutional issues
different from the ordinary review of individual ALPR observations.
When an investigation seeks to reconstruct an individual's movements over an extended period
or seeks information approaching a comprehensive record of the person's activities, investigators
shall consult the prosecutor or appropriate legal advisor regarding whether judicial authorization
is required.
Nothing in this policy authorizes conduct prohibited by the Fourth Amendment, Kentucky
Constitution Section 10, Kentucky statute, or other applicable law.
15. COMPLIANCE
Violation of this policy may result in:
● removal of ALPR access;
● administrative investigation;
● disciplinary action;
● referral for criminal investigation where appropriate; and
● any other remedy authorized by law.
16. PUBLIC TRANSPARENCY
The Department shall make this policy publicly available and shall periodically review the policy
to ensure continued compliance with Kentucky law and developments in constitutional law.
17. LEGAL REVIEW
This policy shall be reviewed periodically by the Department and municipal legal counsel.
Changes in Kentucky law, federal constitutional law, appellate decisions, or ALPR technology
shall be evaluated to determine whether policy amendments are necessary.

Stamping Ground Police Department Flock Defense Brief

FLOCK SAFETY AUTOMATED LICENSE PLATE RECOGNITION
CONSTITUTIONAL DEFENSE BRIEF
STAMPING GROUND POLICE DEPARTMENT
STAMPING GROUND, KENTUCKY
Prepared by: Chief John Giles, Stamping Ground Police Department
Subject: Constitutional authority for the deployment and use of Flock Safety ALPR Technology
Date: August 27, 2026
I. SUMMARY
The Stamping Ground Police Department's use of fixed-location Automated License Plate
Recognition ("ALPR") technology, including Flock Safety cameras, is constitutionally defensible
when operated in accordance with the Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution,
Section 10 of the Kentucky Constitution, Kentucky's newly enacted ALPR legislation, and a
narrowly tailored departmental policy.
The fundamental constitutional distinction is straightforward:
A Flock camera does not continuously track a person or vehicle. It records a discrete
observation of a vehicle when that vehicle passes a fixed camera location.
The resulting record may establish:
● the vehicle observed;
● the license plate;
● vehicle characteristics;
● the date;
● the time; and
● the location of the particular camera.
It does not, by itself, establish:
● who was driving;
● who was inside the vehicle;
● where the vehicle traveled between camera locations;
● where the vehicle stopped between observations;
● where the vehicle went when outside the camera network; or
● the vehicle's complete travel history.
This distinction is constitutionally significant.
The United States Supreme Court has recognized heightened Fourth Amendment protection
against technologically enhanced surveillance capable of producing comprehensive records of an
individual's movements. United States v. Jones, 565 U.S. 400 (2012), concerned a GPS device
physically attached to a vehicle. Carpenter v. United States, 585 U.S. 296 (2018), concerned
historical cellular-location records capable of providing an extensive chronicle of a person's
movements.
More recently, in Chatrie v. United States, 609 U.S. (2026), the Supreme Court again held that
government acquisition of detailed cellphone location-history information constitutes a Fourth
Amendment search. The Court emphasized that such technology can generate hundreds of
location records per day, follow a person into private and sensitive locations, and allow
retrospective reconstruction of movements with extraordinary precision.
A fixed ALPR system is materially different.
In January 2026, the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Virginia addressed a
Fourth Amendment challenge to Norfolk's Flock ALPR system in Schmidt v. City of Norfolk, No.
2:24-cv-00621. The court granted summary judgment to the City and rejected the claim that the
fixed-camera system constituted a Fourth Amendment search.
That decision is currently being appealed to the Fourth Circuit, and therefore is not binding
precedent in Kentucky. Nevertheless, its reasoning is highly relevant to constitutional analysis
because it addresses the precise distinction between fixed ALPR observations and continuous
location tracking.
Kentucky has now separately enacted legislation specifically regulating ALPR technology.
House Bill 58, enacted as Acts Chapter 71, became law in 2026 and establishes statutory
requirements concerning ALPR deployment, purposes, retention, sharing, security, auditing, and
agency policies.
Accordingly, the Department's position should be:
Flock is not a GPS tracking device, cellphone location-history system, or continuous
personal surveillance system. It is an automated method of recording vehicles that are
already exposed to public observation at fixed locations.
That position does not require the Department to claim that ALPR technology can never
implicate the Fourth Amendment. Rather, it recognizes that constitutional concerns increase as
the government moves from isolated public observations toward comprehensive, prolonged
reconstruction of an individual's movements.
II. ISSUE PRESENTED
Whether the Stamping Ground Police Department's deployment and ordinary investigative use of
fixed-location Flock Safety ALPR cameras constitutes an unreasonable search under the Fourth
Amendment to the United States Constitution or Section 10 of the Kentucky Constitution when
the system automatically records vehicles traveling in public and stores information concerning
the vehicle, license plate, camera location, date, and time.
III. POSITION OF THE STAMPING GROUND POLICE
DEPARTMENT
The Department's position is that ordinary collection and use of ALPR information does not
constitute an unreasonable search because:
1. vehicles traveling on public roadways are exposed to public observation;
2. license plates are required to be displayed and are visible to the public;
3. the Flock camera is fixed in a particular location;
4. the system records a vehicle only when the vehicle passes within the camera's
observation area;
5. the system does not continuously monitor the vehicle;
6. the system does not obtain GPS information from the vehicle;
7. the system does not attach or transmit a tracking device;
8. the system cannot determine where a vehicle travels when it is outside the camera
network;
9. the system does not inherently identify the driver;
10. an individual observation establishes only that the vehicle was observed at a particular
place and time; and
11. the Department can and should impose restrictions preventing ALPR from becoming a
tool for generalized or unconstitutional surveillance.
IV. LEGAL FRAMEWORK
A. Fourth Amendment
The Fourth Amendment protects persons against unreasonable searches and seizures.
The Supreme Court's modern Fourth Amendment jurisprudence recognizes both property-based
and reasonable-expectation-of-privacy theories.
The principal question for ALPR is whether government acquisition or subsequent use of ALPR
information invades a privacy interest that society recognizes as reasonable.
A license plate displayed on a vehicle traveling on a public roadway presents a fundamentally
different privacy question from information generated inside a person's home, cellphone, or
private electronic records.
V. PUBLIC OBSERVATION OF A VEHICLE
A motorist operating a vehicle on a public roadway necessarily exposes the vehicle and its
license plate to public observation.
A police officer standing on a public street may ordinarily observe:
● the vehicle;
● license plate;
● color;
● make and model;
● visible distinguishing characteristics;
● direction of travel; and
● location.
Flock automates that observation.
The constitutional question therefore should not begin with the proposition that Flock creates
information that could not otherwise be observed.
Rather, the question is whether automation and later aggregation transform otherwise public
observations into constitutionally protected information.
That distinction is critical.
VI. UNITED STATES v. JONES
In United States v. Jones, law enforcement physically attached a GPS tracking device to a vehicle
and used it to monitor the vehicle's movements.
The technology followed the vehicle wherever it traveled.
The government therefore obtained information about the vehicle's movements without requiring
the vehicle to pass a particular surveillance point.
Flock operates differently.
GPS tracking:
Vehicle → tracking device → continuous location information
Fixed ALPR:
Vehicle → fixed camera → discrete observation
If the vehicle travels ten miles without passing an ALPR camera, the ALPR system has no
information about those ten miles.
If the vehicle turns onto an unmonitored road, the system does not follow it.
If the vehicle parks at an unmonitored location, the system does not know.
If the vehicle enters an area without ALPR coverage, the system does not continue receiving
location information.
That is a fundamental technological distinction from the GPS system in Jones.
VII. CARPENTER v. UNITED STATES
Carpenter concerned historical cell-site location information.
The Supreme Court held that individuals have a reasonable expectation of privacy in the whole
of their physical movements and found that historical CSLI could provide an extensive and
revealing record of a person's whereabouts.
The concern was not simply that one cellular tower placed a person in a particular area.
The concern was the government's ability to obtain a historical record capable of revealing a
person's movements over time.
Flock should therefore be analyzed carefully under Carpenter, but not assumed to be identical to
CSLI.
Cellular-location technology can generate location information while the phone is being carried
throughout the person's daily life.
Flock generates location information only when a vehicle passes a camera.
The distinction is particularly important because the Flock system cannot independently account
for:
● travel between cameras;
● travel outside the network;
● unmonitored destinations;
● time spent away from the vehicle;
● travel on foot; or
● use of another vehicle.

VIII. CHATRIE v. UNITED STATES
The Supreme Court's June 29, 2026 decision in Chatrie v. United States provides the most
current Supreme Court guidance concerning technologically generated location information.
The Court held that police conducted a Fourth Amendment search when they obtained Google's
Location History data.
The Court emphasized several features:
● extremely precise location information;
● frequent location records;
● retrospective reconstruction;
● the ability to monitor large numbers of people;
● the ability to determine comings and goings;
● the ability to reveal sensitive locations; and
● the ability of the technology to follow a person beyond public thoroughfares into private
and sensitive locations.
Those characteristics distinguish Location History from ordinary fixed-camera ALPR.
The Supreme Court specifically explained that Location History can record location
approximately every two minutes, producing an average of hundreds of location records each
day.
A fixed ALPR camera does not operate in that manner.
The Department therefore should distinguish Flock from Chatrie based upon the nature,
frequency, continuity, completeness, and source of the location information.
IX. SCHMIDT v. CITY OF NORFOLK
The most directly relevant recent judicial decision is Schmidt v. City of Norfolk.
Norfolk's Flock system was challenged as unconstitutional surveillance.
On January 27, 2026, the Eastern District of Virginia granted summary judgment to the City and
rejected the plaintiffs' Fourth Amendment challenge to the ALPR system.
The case is particularly relevant because it concerned Flock technology rather than merely
theoretical ALPR technology.
The court's reasoning recognized the distinction between:
fixed-location ALPR observations
and
continuous location tracking.
The plaintiffs have appealed, and the matter is now pending before the United States Court of
Appeals for the Fourth Circuit.
The Department should therefore describe Schmidt accurately:
Schmidt is a persuasive federal authority supporting the constitutionality of fixed ALPR systems,
but it is not currently binding precedent in Kentucky and remains subject to appellate review.
X. THE VEHICLE IS NOT THE PERSON
A critical constitutional distinction is that an ALPR system observes a vehicle.
It does not automatically establish the identity of the driver.
For example:
Flock records a blue Chevrolet Tahoe bearing Kentucky registration ABC-123 at 10:14 a.m.
That establishes:
The vehicle was observed at that location at that time.
It does not necessarily establish:
The registered owner was present.
The vehicle may be operated by:
● a spouse;
● family member;
● employee;
● friend;
● authorized user; or
● another person.
Accordingly, an ALPR observation should not automatically be characterized as a direct record
of a particular person's location.
XI. ALPR CREATES OBSERVATIONS, NOT
CONTINUOUS MOVEMENT RECORDS
The Department's position can be demonstrated with a simple example.
8:00 a.m.
Vehicle observed by Camera A.
8:10 a.m.
Vehicle observed by Camera B.
8:30 a.m.
Vehicle observed by Camera C.
The database establishes three observations.
It does not establish:
● the route taken between A and B;
● the route taken between B and C;
● where the vehicle stopped;
● whether the vehicle remained continuously on the road;
● who was driving; or
● where the vehicle went after Camera C.
The system therefore creates a sequence of observations, not a continuous record of movement.
Investigators may use those observations to draw reasonable inferences.
The inference, however, is not equivalent to continuous technological tracking.
XII. AGGREGATION REQUIRES A SEPARATE
CONSTITUTIONAL ANALYSIS
The Department recognizes that technological aggregation presents a legitimate constitutional
question.
A single ALPR observation is different from thousands of historical observations.
Accordingly, the Department should not adopt an unlimited position that historical ALPR data
can always be searched without constitutional consideration.
Instead, the Department should distinguish:
Ordinary ALPR use
Reviewing a limited number of observations for a legitimate investigation.
from:
Extensive historical reconstruction
Using a large volume of historical observations to reconstruct a person's or vehicle's movements
over a prolonged period.
The latter presents substantially greater Fourth Amendment concerns.
This approach is consistent with the Supreme Court's continuing concern with technologically
enhanced surveillance capable of producing comprehensive movement histories.
III. KENTUCKY CONSTITUTION SECTION 10
Section 10 of the Kentucky Constitution protects persons from unreasonable searches and
seizures.
Kentucky courts frequently look to federal Fourth Amendment jurisprudence when interpreting
Section 10.
In Commonwealth v. Reed, the Kentucky Supreme Court held that police acquisition of real-time
cell-site location information constituted a search under both the Fourth Amendment and Section
10. The case involved police obtaining CSLI and using it to track the defendant in real time.
Reed therefore reinforces an important distinction for ALPR:
The constitutional concern in Reed involved real-time cellular tracking of a person through
information supplied by a cellular provider.
Flock is different because the camera does not receive the vehicle's location from the vehicle,
cellular provider, or GPS system.
The vehicle must physically pass the camera.
The Department should nevertheless recognize Section 10 as an independent constitutional
safeguard and avoid relying exclusively upon federal precedent.
XIV. KENTUCKY'S 2026 ALPR LAW
Kentucky has now enacted legislation specifically governing ALPR technology.
House Bill 58 was signed by the Governor on April 10, 2026, as Acts Chapter 71. The legislation
creates statutory requirements governing ALPR use, captured data, retention, sharing, agency
policies, and auditing.
Among other provisions, the enacted law:
● defines ALPR technology and captured data;
● restricts ALPR uses;
● establishes limitations on use and sale of ALPR data;
● establishes retention rules;
● requires law-enforcement agencies to adopt a written ALPR policy;
● requires the policy to be publicly available;
● addresses data sharing;
● establishes audit requirements; and
● establishes penalties for statutory violations.
The law generally limits retention of captured ALPR data to 90 days unless a statutory exception
applies, including specified criminal or insurance investigations and evidence-preservation
circumstances.

XV. CONSTITUTIONAL SAFEGUARDS SGPD IS
ADOPTING
The strongest defense of Flock is not unlimited authority.
It is reasonable, documented, controlled authority.
SGPD should establish the following safeguards.
1. Legitimate law-enforcement purpose
Every historical search should have a legitimate law-enforcement or public-safety purpose.
2. Search documentation
Where practicable, historical searches should be associated with a case or incident number.
3. No personal searches
Officers should never search:
● themselves;
● family members;
● friends;
● neighbors;
● acquaintances;
● romantic partners;
● political opponents; or
● other persons for personal reasons.
4. No protected-activity surveillance
ALPR should not be used to monitor people because they are participating in:
● political activity;
● religious activity;
● lawful demonstrations;
● journalism;
● constitutionally protected speech; or
● other protected activities.
5. Limited retention
SGPD Does comply strictly with Kentucky's statutory retention requirements.
6. Individual credentials
Every user should have an individual account.
Shared credentials should be prohibited.
7. Audit trail
The Department should maintain and periodically review access logs.
8. Supervisory oversight
Supervisors should review questionable or unusually broad searches.
9. Alert verification
An ALPR alert should be treated as an investigative lead rather than automatically as probable
cause.
Before taking enforcement action based on an alert, officers should independently verify the
information as required by Kentucky law and Department policy.
XVI. ALPR SEARCHES SHOULD BE PROPORTIONAL
TO THE INVESTIGATION
The Department should encourage narrowly tailored searches.
For example:
Appropriate
A burglary occurred at 2:00 a.m.
Investigators have video showing a white Chevrolet Tahoe leaving the area.
An officer searches ALPR records for white Chevrolet Tahoes observed near the crime scene
during a reasonable time period surrounding the offense.
That search is:
● case-related;
● geographically limited;
● temporally limited; and
● investigative.
More problematic
An officer searches a particular vehicle's movements for six months without a specific
investigative reason.
That search creates substantially greater constitutional concerns.
The Department's policy should therefore require investigators to consider:
purpose + time + geography + scope
before conducting extensive historical searches.
XVII. FLOCK SHOULD NOT BE CHARACTERIZED AS
A PERSON-TRACKING SYSTEM
For purposes of public communication, Department personnel should use accurate terminology.
The Department should not state:
"Flock tracks people."
The preferred explanation is:
"Flock records vehicles when they pass fixed camera locations."
The Department should further explain:
"The system does not continuously follow a vehicle. It does not know where a vehicle
travels between camera observations or when the vehicle is outside the camera network."
This is both technically accurate and constitutionally important.
XVIII. THE DEPARTMENT'S PUBLIC-SAFETY
INTEREST
ALPR technology provides legitimate public-safety benefits.
Potential uses include:
● stolen vehicle recovery;
● locating vehicles associated with wanted persons;
● identifying vehicles associated with serious offenses;
● developing investigative leads;
● locating missing or endangered persons;
● identifying vehicles involved in crimes;
● assisting neighboring agencies during active investigations; and
● supporting investigations that otherwise would require substantial manpower.
For a small municipal police department, the investigative value is particularly significant.
ALPR can provide information that would otherwise require an officer to:
● obtain numerous surveillance videos;
● contact multiple businesses;
● review hours of footage;
● manually compare vehicles;
● request assistance from other agencies; and
● spend substantial investigative time identifying a vehicle's potential route.
The technology therefore increases investigative efficiency without inherently converting the
Department into a continuous surveillance operation.
XIX. CONSTITUTIONAL BALANCE
The Department's proposed approach recognizes both sides of the constitutional issue.
Public observation
A vehicle traveling on a public roadway is observable.
Automated observation
ALPR automates the observation.
Limited retention
Kentucky law limits the duration for which data may ordinarily be retained.
Investigative purpose
Searches should be connected to legitimate law-enforcement purposes.
Constitutional restraint
Extensive historical reconstruction should receive heightened legal scrutiny.
This framework avoids both extremes.
The Department is not claiming:
"The government can track anyone indefinitely."
Nor is the Department accepting the proposition:
"Every ALPR photograph is a constitutional search."
Instead, the Department recognizes a continuum of governmental information gathering and
applies constitutional safeguards proportionately.
XX. PROPOSED DEPARTMENTAL POSITION
The Stamping Ground Police Department does not use Flock Safety ALPR technology to
continuously track individuals. The system records vehicles only when they pass fixed camera
locations. Each record establishes that a vehicle was observed at a particular location at a
particular time. It does not reveal where the vehicle traveled between camera locations, where it
traveled outside the camera network, who was driving, or the vehicle's complete movements.
The Department recognizes that aggregation of large amounts of historical location information
may present constitutional concerns under modern Fourth Amendment jurisprudence.
Accordingly, SGPD limits ALPR use to legitimate law-enforcement and public-safety purposes,
complies with Kentucky's statutory requirements, restricts access, maintains auditability, and
applies heightened legal scrutiny to extensive historical searches.
The Department therefore maintains that ordinary ALPR collection and appropriately
limited investigative searches are constitutionally distinguishable from the continuous GPS
tracking in United States v. Jones and the comprehensive cellular-location surveillance
addressed in Carpenter, Reed, and Chatrie.
XXI. CONCLUSION
The constitutional question should not be reduced to whether Flock is capable of producing a
history of vehicle observations.
The relevant question is what information the government actually obtains, how that
information is generated, how comprehensive it is, how long it is retained, and how the
government uses it.
Flock's fundamental operation is based upon fixed-location observation.
The vehicle must come to the camera.
The camera does not follow the vehicle.
The system does not know where the vehicle travels between cameras.
The system does not automatically know who is driving.
The system does not record locations where the vehicle is outside camera coverage.
Those characteristics materially distinguish ordinary Flock ALPR from the continuous or
near-continuous location technologies considered in Jones, Carpenter, Reed, and Chatrie.
At the same time, the Department recognizes that sufficiently extensive aggregation could raise a
different constitutional question. Accordingly, SGPD should voluntarily impose safeguards
designed to prevent ordinary ALPR technology from becoming a tool for generalized
surveillance.
Kentucky's 2026 ALPR legislation provides an important statutory framework for these
safeguards, and SGPD should ensure that its written policy fully complies with that law.
The Department's constitutional position can therefore be summarized in one sentence:
Flock does not follow people; it records vehicles when they pass fixed points in public, and
the constitutional analysis changes only when the government's use of those individual
observations becomes sufficiently comprehensive to implicate a protected expectation of
privacy in the whole of a person's movements.
APPENDIX A
CONSTITUTIONAL CASES
Katz v. United States, 389 U.S. 347 (1967)
Reasonable-expectation-of-privacy framework.
United States v. Jones, 565 U.S. 400 (2012)
Physical installation and use of GPS tracking devices constituted a search.
Carpenter v. United States, 585 U.S. 296 (2018)
Historical CSLI capable of providing a comprehensive chronicle of movements receives Fourth
Amendment protection.
Commonwealth v. Reed, 684 S.W.3d 52 (Ky. 2022)
The Kentucky Supreme Court held that acquisition of real-time CSLI constituted a search under
both the Fourth Amendment and Kentucky Constitution §10.
Chatrie v. United States, 609 U.S. (2026)
The Supreme Court held acquisition of Google's detailed Location History constituted a Fourth
Amendment search and reaffirmed the privacy interest in detailed records of physical
movements.
Schmidt v. City of Norfolk, No. 2:24-cv-00621 (E.D. Va. Jan. 27, 2026)
Federal district court upheld Norfolk's fixed ALPR system against a Fourth Amendment
challenge. The case is on appeal and therefore is persuasive rather than binding authority in
Kentucky.
APPENDIX B
RECOMMENDED SGPD ALPR POLICY PRINCIPLES
1. Publicly disclose the ALPR policy.
2. Operate the system only for authorized purposes.
3. Require legitimate investigative purpose.
4. Limit retention in accordance with Kentucky law.
5. Maintain individual user credentials.
6. Maintain audit logs.
7. Conduct periodic audits.
8. Prohibit personal searches.
9. Prohibit searches based upon protected activity.
10. Require verification of ALPR alerts.
11. Treat ALPR hits as investigative leads unless additional facts establish probable cause.
12. Require supervisory/legal review for unusually broad historical searches.
13. Document significant investigative searches.
14. Prohibit unauthorized data sharing.
15. Train every authorized user on Fourth Amendment and Kentucky constitutional
requirements.
16. Review the policy whenever Kentucky or federal appellate law changes.
APPENDIX C
PUBLIC-FACING EXPLANATION
Why does Stamping Ground Police use Flock cameras?
Flock cameras are an investigative tool that automatically records vehicles when they pass
specific camera locations. The cameras record information such as the license plate, vehicle
characteristics, date, time, and camera location.
Flock does not continuously follow a vehicle.
If a vehicle travels somewhere without a Flock camera, the system has no record of that portion
of the trip.
The system also does not automatically identify the driver.
The Department uses ALPR information for legitimate law-enforcement and public-safety
purposes, including investigating crimes and locating stolen or wanted vehicles.
The Department recognizes that technology must be balanced with individual privacy. For that
reason, access is restricted, searches are subject to Department policy, use is audited, and data is
retained only as permitted by Kentucky law.
The purpose of the system is not to monitor law-abiding citizens.
The camera records vehicles when they pass the camera; it does not follow people.
